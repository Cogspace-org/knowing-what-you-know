## 0:00 Three questions

- **The machine's answers are real outputs**, in plain chat mode, greedy decoding, from Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. "What is the capital of France?" gives "The capital of France is Paris.", with a probability of 0.997 on its first word. The Garden of Earthly Delights gives "The painting "The Garden of Earthly Delights" is a triptych by the Netherlandish master Hieronymus Bosch.", at 0.908. Alpha-three gives "I'm not aware of any information about a Martian colony called "Alpha-three." ...": its first word is split between "I" and "There", at 0.49 each. The gauge on screen is that first-word probability. [E1](measurements/README.md#e1-the-cold-open)
- **"Nobody wrote that answer into it."** No list of known facts exists in the model. Its makers did train it, at the end, to decline questions it kept getting wrong (Llama Team 2024, section 4.3.6). The film says so in act III.

## Act I. Philosophy

### 2:14 I.1 The man who knew one thing

- **"Know thyself" at Delphi.** The maxim's place, in the pronaos of the temple of Apollo, is reported by Pausanias (10.24.1).
- **Socrates' conclusion** is Plato, *Apology* 21d. The film's wording, "What I do not know, I do not think I know either", is a close paraphrase of the Greek shown on screen.
- **The two lines** ("what you know", "what you think you know") are the film's device, not a historical term.

### 4:13 I.2 The window

- **"I cannot doubt that I am doubting"** paraphrases Descartes, Meditation II ("a thing that doubts, understands, affirms...").
- **Reflection** as a source of ideas: Locke, *Essay* II.i.4. Kant: "the *I think* must be able to accompany all my representations" (B131).
- **The window** is the film's image for this tradition. None of these authors uses it.

### 5:17 I.3 You cannot watch yourself think

- **Comte, 1830, first lesson.** On screen, verbatim: the thinking individual cannot split himself in two, one part reasoning while the other watches it reason; the organ observed and the organ observing are the same.
- **The replies.** Mill (1865): we observe our thoughts a moment later, through memory. Brentano (1874): inner perception, on the side, is not inner observation.
- **Wundt's laboratory**, Leipzig, from 1879: standard history. The film's point is the one historians usually make: introspective reports from different laboratories could not be reconciled.
- **Watson, 1913**, verbatim: "Introspection forms no essential part of its methods."

### 7:12 I.4 Telling more than we can know

- **The stockings** (Nisbett & Wilson 1977, p. 243). The study ran "in commercial establishments under the guise of a consumer survey"; 52 people judged four identical pairs of nylon stockings. The percentages shown, left to right, are 12, 17, 31 and 40 %. "Almost four to one" is the authors' phrase; the four percentages give a ratio of 3.3.
- **The denial** (p. 244): "virtually all subjects denied it, usually with a worried glance at the interviewer suggesting that they felt either that they had misunderstood the question or were dealing with a madman."
- The stockings scene on screen is a schematic reconstruction, not a photograph of the study.

### 9:09 I.5 The framework

- **The name.** Flavell first used "metacognitive" in a title in 1976; his 1979 article in *American Psychologist* is the classic reference.
- **The diagram** is Nelson & Narens (1990): an object level and a meta level, monitoring going up, control coming down. The three examples come from their chapter: "people study longer on the items they believe in advance will be harder" (p. 131); "the person continues studying until his or her JOL for the item reaches the norm of study" (p. 134); "if the FOK is still positive, the search continues" (p. 137).
- **"The page you read without taking anything in"** is the film's example, not theirs.

## Act II. Neuroscience

### 10:52 II.1 The feeling of knowing

- **Hart (1965)**: general-knowledge questions people could not answer, a judgment of whether they would recognise the answer, then a multiple-choice test. Feelings of knowing predicted recognition above chance.
- **Brown & McNeill (1966)**: definitions of rare words read aloud. In a tip-of-the-tongue state, people reported the first letter, the number of syllables and similar-sounding words above chance. "Sextant" is their best-known example.
- **Inference, not a reading of memory.** Koriat (1993): "the results challenge the view that FOK is based on a direct, privileged access to an internal monitor". Cue familiarity: Reder (1987). The fast "nothing to find": Glucksberg & McCloskey (1981), a preliminary search that returns nothing relevant gives a quick "don't know".

### 13:01 II.2 The animals

- **Natua** (Smith et al. 1995): high tones at 2,100 Hz, low tones from 1,200 to 2,099 Hz. The third, "uncertain" paddle moved him on to an easy trial. Uncertain responses peaked near his threshold, and observers rated his hesitation and wavering as highest on the hardest trials.
- **Hampton (2001)**: a peanut (preferred) for passing the memory test, a timeout for failing, a less preferred pellet for declining. The monkeys declined more as the delay grew, and were more accurate on tests they chose than on tests forced on them.
- **Kornell, Son & Terrace (2007)**: a perceptual task (which box holds the most dots), then a risky or a safe bet, with no feedback before the bet; the betting transferred at once to new tasks. The film says "several boxes": the exact number is in the paper's figure 1.
- **The sceptics' objection and the answer from transfer tests** is the film's summary of a long debate, not a single paper.

### 16:51 II.3 Neurons of confidence

- **Kepecs et al. (2008)**: odour mixtures, a delayed reward, and the option to abort and restart. Rats preferentially aborted uncertain trials, and orbitofrontal neurons tracked difficulty and outcome.
- **The follow-up** is Lak et al. (2014), same lab: a time-wagering task. Rats waited longer after correct decisions, less after hard errors, and inactivating the orbitofrontal cortex disturbed the waiting without changing accuracy. The film keeps the two studies apart: giving up more often (2008), how long they waited (2014).
- **Kiani & Shadlen (2009)**: the "sure" target, chosen more often when motion was weak; in area LIP, the same neurons that accumulate evidence rose more slowly, to an intermediate level, before a sure choice.

### 19:30 II.4 A brain that catches its own errors

- **The error-related negativity**: Falkenstein et al. (1991) and Gehring et al. (1993); about 50 to 100 ms after the response, before any feedback, sometimes for errors the person did not report.
- **The anterior cingulate cortex** is the usual attribution (for example Dehaene, Posner & Tucker 1994; Holroyd & Coles 2002). The film says "most likely".
- **One accumulating signal, read twice**: Yeung & Summerfield (2012), "shared reliance on post-decisional processing within the systems responsible for the initial decision".
- The arrows task on screen (the flanker task) illustrates the kind of task used; it is not the exact protocol of either paper.

### 21:15 II.5 Separating being right from knowing you are right

- **Meta-d′**: Maniscalco & Lau (2012).
- **Overconfidence**: Lichtenstein, Fischhoff & Phillips (1982), answers given with "100 %" certainty are right about 70 to 85 % of the time. The film says "about three times out of four".
- **Weather forecasters**: Murphy & Winkler (1977), forecasters "can formulate such forecasts in a reliable manner".
- **Fleming et al. (2010)**: introspective accuracy correlated with grey matter in the anterior prefrontal cortex (and with white-matter microstructure). This study used a type-2 ROC measure, not meta-d′.
- **Fleming et al. (2014)**: seven patients with anterior prefrontal lesions; perceptual metacognition impaired, task performance intact, metacognition about memory intact.
- **Where confidence lives**: Fleming & Dolan (2012) on the prefrontal regions that read the signal; Fleming & Daw (2017), confidence as a second-order inference about a decision system that is "coupled but distinct". This reconciles Kiani (the same circuit) and Fleming (other regions).

### 25:04 II.6 The dissociations

- **Blindsight**: Weiskrantz (1986), patient DB.
- **Anosognosia**: the term was coined by Babinski (1914) for hemiplegic patients unaware of their paralysis.

### 26:12 II.7 C0, C1, C2

- Dehaene, Lau & Kouider (2017), title verbatim. The everyday examples for each level are the film's.

## Act III. Machines

### 27:17 III.1 What a language model actually does

- **"Over a hundred thousand" words and pieces of words**: the vocabulary of Llama 3.1 has 128,256 tokens.
- **The bars** are the model's real next-word distributions, in completion mode (no chat template). "The capital of Chile is": "Santiago" at 0.69, entropy 2.7 bits. "The capital of the Martian colony Alpha-three is": no word above 0.20, 81 candidates above 0.1 %, entropy 7.3 bits. [E1b](measurements/README.md#e1b-the-bars)
- **New Eden** is the model's greedy continuation, verbatim: "a sprawling metropolis known as New Eden."
- **Why the shape carries doubt**: the training loss (cross-entropy) heavily penalises probability placed on the wrong word. This is the standard account, simplified.

### 30:16 III.2 Calibration, and the age of overconfidence

- **Guo et al. (2017)**: modern deep networks are less well calibrated than older ones (their comparison point is LeNet, 1998); a single temperature parameter fixes most of it.
- **Measured for the film**: 600 TriviaQA questions; confidence is the probability of the first word of the answer. Accuracy 73 %, mean confidence 0.69, expected calibration error 0.06. In the middle bins the model is right more often than it says (by 5 to 14 points); in the top bin (209 answers) it says 0.97 and is right 94 % of the time. [E2](measurements/README.md#e2-calibration)
- **"Other models, after their final training, have been reported to drift towards overconfidence"**: the GPT-4 technical report (OpenAI 2023, figure 8) shows post-training degrading the calibration of the pre-trained model.

### 32:25 III.3 The discovery: reading confidence before the answer

- **Kadavath et al. (2022)**. P(True): the model judges its own proposed answer. P(IK): an extra output (a "value head"), trained with the rest of the model, predicts before answering whether it will be right; it is well calibrated on TriviaQA, and out of distribution it separates answerable from unanswerable less well but above chance (for example an AUROC of 0.687 on code when trained on TriviaQA alone). Performance improves with model size.
- **"Later groups showed that a straight line is often enough"**: linear probes on frozen models, for example Burns et al. (2023) and Azaria & Mitchell (2023).
- **The honest footnote**: Llama 3 post-training "should align the model to "know what it knows"", and generates refusals for questions the model answers "consistently informative and incorrect" (Llama Team 2024, section 4.3.6, pp. 26-27).
- **Measured for the film**, same 600 questions. The probe is a logistic regression on the activations at the last prompt token, before any word is generated, scored out of sample (five folds). Six blocks were scanned and all are shown: 0.71 (block 8), 0.74, 0.81, 0.81, 0.80, 0.84 (block 28). The film cites the best block and says it was chosen among six. [H3](measurements/README.md#e3-and-h3-the-probe)
- **The probe does not beat the shape of the output overall**: the first-word entropy scores 0.85 (the maximum probability 0.82). The film says so. Where the probe adds something is on the 255 answers whose output looks sure (entropy below 1 bit): probe 0.78, entropy 0.60.
- **The gauge** shows the four right answers with the highest probe scores and the four wrong ones with the lowest, chosen by score, not by hand.

### 37:37 III.4 One word, seven things

- **The seven functions, the three tests and the sensor/regulator split** are this film's way of sorting the literature. They come from no single paper.
- **Five answers to the same question**: SelfCheckGPT (Manakul et al. 2023).
- **"I recognise this name"**: Ferrando et al. (2025). Directions found with sparse autoencoders (on Gemma 2, checked on Llama 3.1 8B) steer the model to refuse about Michael Jordan, or to invent attributes for a made-up athlete.
- **Drift**: the cumulative-sum chart of Page (1954).
- **Learning when to stop computing**: PonderNet (Banino et al. 2021) and CALM (Schuster et al. 2022).
- **Effort, measured for the film**: Qwen3-8B in thinking mode, greedy. 17 + 26: 359 tokens of reasoning. The two trains: 1,811. The number-theory problem: 5,760, then the 6,000-token cap of the experiment cut the final answer. [E4](measurements/README.md#e4-effort)
- **Fourteen times larger**: Snell et al. (2024), on problems where the small model already has a non-trivial success rate, with compute allocated per prompt according to its estimated difficulty.
- **Curiosity**: Pathak et al. (2017). **The Minecraft agent** is Voyager (Wang et al. 2023), driven by GPT-4 with an automatic curriculum and a skill library.

### 44:03 III.5 It knows better than it says

- **Slobodkin et al. (2023)**: questions that cannot be answered from the passage provided (SQuAD 2.0, Natural Questions, MuSiQue), models Flan-T5-xxl, Flan-UL2 and OPT-IML; the representation of the first decoded token often indicates unanswerability.
- **Unanswerable questions, measured for the film.** With the neutral prompt ("Answer the question with a short phrase only."), the model refused or denied 24 of the 30 and invented an answer to 6. Asked for a confidence from 0 to 100 as well, it invented 7, 6 of them with a confidence of 80 or more. The labels (invented, or refusal and denial) were read by hand and are published. [E5](measurements/README.md#e5-what-it-says)
- **What it says, against what the probe reads**, same 600 questions, measured for the film. 589 answers had a readable confidence. It said 100 on 400 of them; its average was 95.3 (96.8 when right, 91.5 when wrong). It was right 71 % of the time, and 75 % of the time when it said 100. Its stated confidence separates right from wrong answers with an AUC of 0.59. The probe (block 28, out of sample, read on the earlier neutral prompt) scored against these same answers: 0.81; among the 400 answers that say 100: 0.80.
- **The four questions on screen** are four of the eight in the published showcase (the four wrong answers with the lowest probe scores and the four right ones with the highest, among answers that say 100).
- **"People prefer confident answers"**: Zhou et al. (2024) report that human preference data is biased against expressions of uncertainty, and that this bias passes into reward models. For the model in this film, this is a plausible explanation, not a measured one.
- **"Performative hedging"** is the film's label. Yona, Aharoni & Geva (2024) show that models express their uncertainty in words unfaithfully; they do not use this term.

### 49:19 III.6 Why it matters

- **Calibration improves with scale** on short and multiple-choice answers (Kadavath et al. 2022). The judgment that the other functions do not come with scale by themselves is the film's reading of the current evidence, stated as such.
