## 16:51 II.3 Neurons of confidence

In two thousand eight, Adam Kepecs and his colleagues found it in a rat.

And now the part that matters. The two thousand eight study looked in the orbitofrontal cortex, a region just behind the eyes. When the monkey chose the sure option, those same neurons had climbed more slowly and stopped at an intermediate level. Think about that. Remember this counter. In the third act, someone will read confidence from inside an artificial network. It will look surprisingly like this.

## 21:15 II.5 Separating being right from knowing you are right

We now have behaviour, and we have neurons. We need one more piece before the machines. A piece of arithmetic: a way to measure metacognition separately from performance. The two are easy to confuse. With machines, that confusion will matter.

Two people can score the same on a test, say eighty percent, and still differ completely in one respect. One of them knows which answers she got wrong. Her confidence drops exactly on those. The other is equally confident on everything, right or wrong.

Psychologists call these type one and type two. Type one: can you tell the stimuli apart? Type two: can you tell your own correct answers from your own mistakes? Same score on type one. Very different on type two.

In two thousand twelve, Brian Maniscalco and Hakwan Lau gave type two a proper scale, called meta-d-prime. Put simply: of the information she used to answer, how much reaches her confidence? So you can finally ask, cleanly: given how good this person is at the task, how good is she at knowing how good she is?

And there is a second question, which sounds like the same one and is not. Sensitivity asks whether your confidence separates your right answers from your wrong ones. Calibration asks whether the numbers are honest. When you say seventy percent, are you right seventy percent of the time?

Draw it. On one axis, what people say. On the other, how often they are right. A perfectly calibrated person sits on the diagonal. Most of us do not. On general-knowledge questions, when people say they are one hundred percent sure, they are right about three times out of four. Overconfidence is the rule, not the exception. Think of the last time you said you were one hundred percent sure. Were you right?

There is a famous exception, though: weather forecasters. When they say seventy percent chance of rain, it rains about seventy percent of the time. Why them? Because they make the same kind of judgement every day, and every day the sky tells them the answer. Feedback builds calibration.

Sensitivity, and calibration: the machines will be judged on both.

And sensitivity, knowing when you are right, has an address in the brain. In two thousand ten, Stephen Fleming and colleagues measured introspective accuracy in a group of people, and scanned their brains. Those with better metacognition had more grey matter in the anterior prefrontal cortex: the very front of the brain, a region that has expanded enormously in humans.

Four years later, the same group studied patients with damage to that region. Their perception was intact. Their performance on the task was normal. But their ability to know when they were wrong was impaired. And, curiously, only for perception: their metacognition about their own memory was untouched. Even the meta level has its own specialised parts.

So which is it? A moment ago, confidence was readable in the very circuit that decides. Now it lives somewhere else. Both are true. The raw signal is computed where the decision is made: how far the counter climbed. Reading that signal, and turning it into a judgment you can use or report, takes other regions, at the front of the brain. And that second step is the one that can break.

Knowing, and knowing that you know, live in different places. And if they live in different places, then one of them can break while the other keeps working.

## 26:12 II.7 C0, C1, C2

## 30:16 III.2 Calibration, and the age of overconfidence

Accurate first. You met the test in act two: calibration. When it says seventy percent, is it right seventy percent of the time?

## 32:25 III.3 The discovery: reading confidence before the answer

So the signal is there, and on simple questions it is honest. The next question is the one from act two: can it be read, from behaviour or from the inside, the way we read the dolphin and the monkey?

In twenty twenty-two, a team of researchers asked exactly that. Their paper was titled Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know. They tried two things, and each one has an echo in act two.

First, they let the model answer a question, then showed it its own answer and asked: is this true? The model gives a probability; they called it P-true. A second look, after the answer. That is the error signal from act two, in a machine. And it was a fairly good judge of its own answers.

Second, the result that should stop you. Could they tell, before the model said anything, whether it knew the answer? They did not ask it in words. They opened it up.

The next part is the most technical of the film. Stay with me. It is also the heart of it. A language model is a stack of layers. At each layer, the input is represented as a long list of numbers: an activation vector. The researchers added one small extra output to the model: a single number, read from that internal state before the model had written a single word. They trained it to predict one thing: will the answer be right? They called it P-I-K, the probability that "I know". That is Hart's feeling of knowing, in a machine.

It worked. The model could predict, in advance, which questions it would get right. On the questions it was trained on, the number was well calibrated. On entirely new kinds of problems, like code or mathematics, it still separated what the model could answer from what it could not: less well, but better than chance. And it got better as the models grew larger. Later groups showed that something even simpler is often enough: a straight line through the activations, with the model itself left untouched. That simple reader is called a probe. The information "I know this" or "I do not know this" was there, in the activations, readable with a ruler, before the model had said anything.

Stop and look at what that is. This is Kiani and Shadlen's idea, in a machine. The confidence is not in a separate module. It is readable in the same circuit that produces the answer, before the answer comes out. The counter, again, this time made of numbers.

And think about where it came from. None of the trillions of words this model was trained on carried a label saying "this is a hard question". The signal was never taught directly. It emerged, from the pressure we saw a moment ago: to be confident only where the text is predictable.

## 37:37 III.4 One word, seven things

This is where the research literature becomes confusing, and I want to slow down, because the confusion is instructive. Once models started showing signs of self-monitoring, the word "metacognition" was applied to everything. A model that says "I think". A confidence score. A system that sends each word to a specialised sub-network. A model that thinks longer on hard problems. All of it, the same word.

So let us impose some discipline. Three tests, taken straight from acts one and two.

One: the signal must be about the model's own state, its activations or its output distribution, not just about the input. A separate program that guesses "this question looks hard" from the question alone is not metacognition. It is not looking inside. It is not a second line about the first.

Two: the signal must be used. It must change what the system does. A confidence score that only a researcher ever reads is a measurement, not a meta level. The dolphin pressed the paddle.

Three: the mechanism must be identifiable: ideally something you could switch off and see what changes. Like Fleming's patients.

With those tests, here is one way to sort the literature: seven distinct functions. Three of them are sensors: they produce a monitoring signal. Four are regulators: they consume it to control behaviour. Nelson and Narens' diagram, once more. Let's walk through them.

Sensor one, calibration. We have seen it. Does seventy percent mean seventy percent? The oldest, the best measured, and still not solved for long free-form answers.

Sensor two, out-of-domain detection. Am I on familiar ground? A family doctor knows when a case is beyond her and refers to a specialist. For a language model, the failure is the hallucination: a fluent, plausible, false answer. You may have seen it yourself: ask for a reference, and it invents one that looks real. New Eden, with a bibliography.

One clever detector: ask the model the same question five times. If it knows, the five answers agree. If it is inventing, they contradict each other. Inconsistency as a symptom of ignorance.

And a striking finding from twenty twenty-five. Inside a language model, researchers found internal directions that seem to mean "I recognise this name", and "I don't". Push the model towards "I don't", and it refuses to answer about Michael Jordan. Push it towards "I do", and it invents facts about an athlete who does not exist. The sense of "I know this" is a thing you can point at. Test three, passed.

Sensor three looks across time. Has the world changed since I learned? The tools come from factory quality control in the nineteen fifties: watch your own error rate, sound the alarm when it drifts. Sounding the alarm is easy. Relearning without forgetting everything else is the hard part. It is like moving house: you notice in a second that the flat is different; relearning the light switches takes weeks.

Three ways to notice. Now, four ways to act on what you noticed: the regulators, the arrow going down. The first: routing. How should I handle this: fast, or carefully? The simplest version is a cascade: a small, cheap model answers; if its confidence is low, the question is passed to a large, expensive one. The sensor feeds the regulator directly. It is the escape paddle, for a computer.

A caution. Some large models also route: they send each word to a different specialised part of the network. But they decide from the word itself, not from any internal sense of difficulty. By our first test, that is not metacognition. Other designs learn an internal signal that says "stop computing here", and that one does cross the line.

Regulator two: effort. Not which path, but how much. Here is a reasoning model, of the kind released in twenty twenty-four and twenty twenty-five. Three problems: a simple sum, a puzzle about two trains, and a hard question in number theory. Watch the length of its draft before the final answer.

Short. Longer. Much longer. For the sum: three hundred and fifty-nine tokens of reasoning. Tokens are pieces of words. For the trains: eighteen hundred. For the number-theory problem: fifty-seven hundred, and there it hit the limit I had set. The model spends more computation where the problem is harder. So do you. Does the model sense the difficulty? Or does a hard problem simply take more steps? That is still an open question.

And this turned out to be a serious lever. In twenty twenty-four, researchers took problems a small model could already solve some of the time. Given more time to think, it could beat a model fourteen times larger. And the best results came from matching the effort to how hard each question looked. The instinct that bigger is always better turned out to be, at least partly, wrong.

Regulator three: exploration. Where should I look for what I am missing? In artificial agents this became "curiosity": go where your own predictions fail, because that is where there is something to learn. An agent dropped into Minecraft gives itself tasks just beyond what it can already do: a wooden pickaxe, then a stone one, then an iron one. It teaches itself to craft its tools.

Regulator four, the last one, and the one that matters most for you. The only one pointed at the human. Honest hedging: phrasing the answer so that it reflects the confidence. "I think." "I'm not certain." "You should check this."

It is the most visible of the seven. It is what a user perceives as humility. It is also the most misleading. And it deserves its own section, because it is where the promise from the first minute gets paid.

## 44:03 III.5 It knows better than it says

Back to the stockings. Humans tell more than they know: the narrator speaks with confidence about a process it cannot see.

Language models, it turns out, have the opposite problem. They know more than they tell.

In twenty twenty-three, Aviv Slobodkin and colleagues gave models questions that could not be answered from the text in front of them. The models answered anyway, fluently, confidently. Hallucinations.

Then they looked inside. And in the activations, as early as the first word of the answer, the model had already encoded that the question was unanswerable. It knew. It said the opposite.

The model from the first minute does it too. On thirty questions that have no answer, it refuses most of them. But six times, it invents an answer. And when we also ask how sure it is, almost every invention comes with eighty or more.

And now, the promise from the first minute. We asked it, on the same six hundred questions, how confident it was, from zero to one hundred. It said one hundred on four hundred of them. Its average was ninety-five. When it was right, it said ninety-seven. When it was wrong, ninety-one.

It was right about seven times in ten. And on the four hundred answers where it said one hundred, it was right three times out of four. That is the human figure from act two. Its next-word numbers were honest. The numbers it says out loud are overconfident, the way ours are.

How well do those numbers separate the right answers from the wrong ones? Barely: point five-nine, where a coin toss is point five. The probe, reading the inside of the same model, on the same questions and the same answers: point eight-one, about what it scored before.

Now look only at the four hundred answers where it said one hundred. There, its words carry no information at all: every one of them says the same thing. And the probe still tells right from wrong: point eight.

Put the three readings side by side. Its words: barely better than a coin toss. The shape of its output: point eight-five, as we saw. The inside: about as good, and still working where the output looks sure.

Four questions. For all four, the model says one hundred. Raging Bull: the probe reads high; correct. A candy-bar slogan: the model says Coca-Cola, the probe reads low; the answer was Snickers. Mull, the Scottish island: high; correct. A footballer with a biblical surname: the model says Geoff Hurst, the probe reads low; wrong. The same word for all four. The probe tells them apart.

The words say almost nothing. The activations say a great deal. And the probe is the one that tracks the truth. We opened the machine, we read its doubt before it spoke, and we caught it saying the opposite.

Why the gap? Part of it is us. Modern models are refined with human feedback: people compare answers and pick the one they prefer. And people prefer confident answers. We all do. Trained on that preference, a model learns to sound sure. The monitoring signal is still inside. What got weaker is the step that puts it into words.

Which brings us to the trap, and back to the two patients from act two. Two models can look identical from outside and be opposite inside. The first always speaks with assurance, yet inside, a signal tracks its errors. Like the blindsight patient, it has the information and does not report it. The second says "I'm not sure" on every answer, by reflex, with no signal behind it. Like the anosognosia patient, its words are cut off from what is going on inside. Only here, the words say doubt instead of "all is well".

Call the second case performative hedging. And nothing rules it out. A model can learn to sprinkle "I think" everywhere, and pass a test of humility, with nothing underneath.

Which is why the rule from act one applies here with full force. The verbal report is not the measurement. To know whether a model knows what it knows, you do not ask it. You open it, and you measure, as Fleming measured his patients, as Kiani read LIP.

And there is an irony here, and it is not a small one. The best measurements, the numbers under the words and the activations behind them, are the ones most users never see. Most of the world talks to these models through a closed interface. The laboratory reads the numbers. The product reads the words.

So what can you do, with only the words? Two things. Treat a confident tone as a report, not a measurement. And when it matters, ask the same question again, in a fresh conversation. When it knows, the answers agree. When it is inventing, they drift apart.

## 49:19 III.6 Why it matters

So let me step back, and ask the only question that matters for the next few years. What should we want from these systems?

Not a model that is always right. That does not exist, for machines or for us. Socrates already knew that. What we should want is a model that knows when it might be wrong, and says so, and acts on it. The dolphin's third paddle.

Seen this way, a hallucination is often not a failure of knowledge. It is a failure of the meta level, of C-two. The signal was there. Nothing listened.

And there is a tempting belief that all of this will simply arrive with bigger models. For most of the seven, the evidence so far does not show it. Calibration does improve with scale, on short answers. Drift is the exception, for a dull reason: if the new world was in the training data, there is nothing left to notice. The rest does not come by itself: knowing when you are on unfamiliar ground, routing, effort, exploration, honest hedging. Larger models are more accurate. They do not, by themselves, tell us more honestly where their limits are. These functions have to be built, measured, and verified, separately.

Open questions remain, and I want to be honest about them. Do these functions emerge at scales we have not reached? How do the seven interact inside one system? And how do we close the gap between what the probes can read and what the products actually expose?

## 50:54 Outro

Socrates claimed one thing: to know what he did not know.

Two and a half thousand years later, a machine built to predict words has learned some of that. Partly. Silently. Mostly without being asked. The knowledge is in its circuits. What it has not learned is to tell us, reliably.

Comte was right about one thing. Nobody can watch themselves think. Not us, not the machine.

But a signal can be measured. In a dolphin's hesitation. In a monkey's neurons. In a straight line through the activations of a network. And in you. The next time a name is on the tip of your tongue, notice that feeling. That is the second line, at work.

Knowing what you know is not only a feeling. It is a function. And functions can be measured.
